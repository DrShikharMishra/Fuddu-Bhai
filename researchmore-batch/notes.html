Day 1 · Protocol

Ask a good question.

Every review that fails, fails here. A vague question gives a vague search, a vague pool and a vague paper. Spend today making the question sharp enough to hurt.

PICOEligibilityPRISMA-PPROSPERO
pairwise first. network last. in that order.

1.1 What are we actually doing?

DesignWhat it isOutputUse when
Narrative reviewAuthor picks and summarises papers; no fixed methodOpinion with citationsTeaching, background. Not evidence.
Systematic review (SR)Pre-specified question, reproducible search, 2 reviewers, critical appraisalStructured synthesisAlways the foundation
Meta-analysis (MA)Statistical pooling of results inside an SROne pooled estimate + CIStudies are similar enough to combine
Network MA (NMA)MA of ≥3 treatments at once using direct + indirect evidenceEvery pairwise effect + rankingMany options, few head-to-head trials
Scoping reviewMaps what exists; no pooling, no RoB neededEvidence mapBroad, exploratory questions
High-yield

Every MA should sit inside an SR. Not every SR needs an MA. A meta-analysis without a systematic search is just arithmetic on a convenient sample of papers.

Where it sits in the evidence hierarchy

Single case → case series → case-control → cohort → RCT → SR/MA of RCTs (top). Garbage-in applies: a pooled estimate from biased trials is a precise wrong answer. That is why Days 3 and 5 exist.

BadWhy it failsSharpened

Reviewer trap
  • Outcome = "outcomes". Name the primary outcome, the measure and the time point. Switching primary outcome after seeing results is outcome-switching: the cardinal sin.
  • Population too broad ("all patients") → heterogeneity you can never explain. Too narrow → 2 studies.
  • Comparator left vague: "standard care" varies by country and decade. Define it.

1.5 The protocol and PRISMA-P

Definition

Protocol = the written plan, fixed before you screen, stating question, eligibility, search, methods of appraisal, synthesis and what you will do if the data are awkward. PRISMA-P (2015) is the 17-item checklist for what a protocol must contain.

Why bother? It stops you choosing methods after peeking at results, it exposes duplicate work, and journals and reviewers increasingly ask for it.

Protocol sectionWhat goes in it
AdministrativeTitle ("systematic review"/"meta-analysis"), registration, authors and roles, amendments, funding
IntroductionRationale, objectives in PICO form
Methods: eligibilityPICOS, years, languages, report status
Methods: searchDatabases, registers, grey sources, draft strategy
Methods: study recordsManagement software, selection process (2 reviewers), data extraction items
Methods: appraisalRoB tool, how judgements are used
Methods: synthesisEffect measure, model, heterogeneity, subgroups, sensitivity, publication bias, certainty (GRADE)

Registering on PROSPERO

  • PROSPERO is a free international registry of systematic reviews with a health-related outcome, maintained by the Centre for Reviews and Dissemination, University of York.
  • Register before you start screening; it is meant to record plans, not completed work. Registration is a record, not peer review or an endorsement.
  • Needs: a title, review question, search plan, eligibility, primary outcome, RoB method, synthesis plan. Keep these identical to your protocol.
  • Not eligible (typically scoping reviews, narrative reviews)? A public time-stamped protocol on OSF Registries is the fallback.
Reviewer trap

Registration after results is theatre. If methods changed, say so in a "deviations from protocol" paragraph and update the registration. Honest change is fine. Hidden change is misconduct.

Hands-on · Day 1 deliverable
  1. Write your topic as a single PICO sentence + one primary outcome with time point.
  2. Search PROSPERO + PubMed for existing reviews; paste the 3 closest and write one line each on how yours differs.
  3. Fill the eligibility table (above) with a reason in every row.
  4. Draft the protocol headings from PRISMA-P and write Introduction + Objectives (150–250 words).

Answer B. PRISMA 2020 = completed review; PRISMA-S = searches; PRISMA-NMA = network reviews.
4. A question about harms of an environmental exposure in cohort studies is best framed as:
A. PICO B. PECO C. SPIDER D. PCC
Answer B. Exposure replaces Intervention when you do not assign it.
5. A meta-analysis without a systematic search is best described as:
A. Acceptable if studies are RCTs B. A narrative review with maths C. A scoping review D. An NMA
Answer B. Selective inclusion biases the pooled estimate however good the statistics.

A. Skewed multiplicative B. Always normal C. Unit-dependent D. Absolute

Find everything.

You are not looking for papers that support you. You are trying to find all the studies, so the missing ones cannot embarrass you later.

2.1 Anatomy of a search

Definition

Sensitivity = proportion of relevant records you retrieve (recall). Precision = proportion of retrieved records that are relevant. A systematic search deliberately buys sensitivity with precision: expect to screen 1,000+ records to include 10.

Build it block by block

  1. Pick the 2–3 PICO concepts that must appear (usually P + I; do not search outcome — it hides studies that report it poorly).
  2. For each concept: controlled vocabulary + free-text synonyms joined by OR.
  3. Join concepts with AND.
  4. Add a design filter only if validated (see below).
  5. Test: does it retrieve 5–10 "golden" papers you already know?
ToolSymbolDoesExample

Field tag[tiab], [mh]Restrict to title/abstract, MeSHinfection[tiab]
Proximity (Embase/Ovid)adjN / NEAR/nWords within n of each otherwound adj3 infection
EXAMPLE (PubMed, illustrative)
#1  "Appendicitis"[mh] OR appendicitis[tiab] OR appendectom*[tiab] OR appendicectom*[tiab]
#2  "Laparoscopy"[mh] OR laparoscop*[tiab] OR "minimally invasive"[tiab]
#3  #1 AND #2
#4  #3 AND (randomized controlled trial[pt] OR randomized[tiab] OR randomised[tiab] OR placebo[tiab] OR trial[tiab])
Reviewer trap
  • Only MeSH → misses new, un-indexed records. Only free text → misses indexed synonyms. Use both.
  • The final line above is a simplified teaching filter. For real reviews use a validated, published RCT filter and cite it.
  • Reporting "searched PubMed and Google" with no strategy, no dates. Reviewers can't reproduce it. Post the full strategy for every database.

Mnemonic

MECCA

2.3 Quality-check the search

  • PRESS (Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies): have a second person (ideally a librarian) check logic, syntax, spelling, missed terms.
  • PRISMA-S: 16-item checklist for reporting searches (databases, dates, full strategies, filters, limits).
  • Record search date, interface (Ovid / PubMed web), and records per source: you need these for the flow diagram.
  • Update the search before submission if >12 months old.

2.4 Deduplicate, then screen with two reviewers

Rayyan workflow (free web tool)

  1. Export from each database as RIS/CSV/PubMed format. Keep the file names and counts.
  2. Create a Rayyan review → upload files → Detect duplicates → resolve (never bulk-delete blindly).
  3. Blind ON so reviewers can't see each other's decisions. Both reviewers screen all records.
  4. Use keyword highlighting: green = include terms, red = exclude terms (speeds, doesn't decide).
  5. Stage 1: titles/abstracts → Include / Exclude / Maybe. Stage 2: full text → Include / Exclude + reason.
  6. Turn blind OFF → resolve conflicts by discussion, third reviewer if stuck.
Clinical pearl

Pilot first. Both reviewers screen the same 50–100 records and compare. If you disagree a lot, your criteria are the problem, not the reviewers. Cohen's κ is often reported for agreement, but the bigger point is the process.

Exclusion reasons must be exact

Use a short, ordered list so each excluded full text gets one main reason: wrong population · wrong intervention · wrong comparator · wrong outcome · wrong study design · duplicate · no full text. These feed the PRISMA flow diagram.

Fig 2.1 PRISMA-style flow with hypothetical numbers. Check the arithmetic at every arrow: 1,842 − 394 = 1,448; 1,448 − 1,386 = 62; 62 − 31 = 31.
Reviewer trap
  • One reviewer screening alone is single-reviewer bias, however experienced.
  • Flow numbers that don't add up, instant credibility loss.
  • Excluding on "no full text" without trying: email the author, use interlibrary loan.
Hands-on · Day 2 deliverable
  1. Build a PubMed strategy for your PICO (2 blocks + free text + MeSH). Test it: does it find your 5 golden papers?
  2. Translate it to a second database (Embase or CENTRAL) and note what syntax changed.
  3. Upload a sample of 100 records into Rayyan with a friend; screen blind; compute % agreement.
  4. Write your protocol's Search and Selection sections.

Day 3 · Evidence

Judge the evidence.

A trial being published doesn't make it trustworthy. Today you extract data cleanly and decide, domain by domain, how likely each result is to be wrong.

Data extractionRoB 2ROBINS-Irobvis
bias ≠ poor reporting. know the difference.

3.1 Data extraction

  1. Build the form before extracting: study ID, design, setting, population, intervention details, comparator, outcome definitions, time points, events/N per arm or mean/SD/N, funding, conflicts.
  2. Pilot on 2–3 studies. Fix the form. Then extract.
  3. Two reviewers independently (or one extracts, one verifies all). Compare. Resolve.
  4. Keep a source note: table/page number for every number.
Reviewer trap
  • Multi-arm trials: don't count the control group twice. Combine arms or split the shared group's N.
  • Multiple reports of one trial → one study. Link them. Counting a trial twice inflates precision.
  • Intention-to-treat vs per-protocol: take ITT where reported, and state which.
  • Medians/IQR to mean/SD: estimates exist but are approximations. Flag them and run a sensitivity analysis.
  • Missing data: contact the author (two attempts). Do not impute without a documented method.
Clinical pearl

Extract the same outcome at the same time point. "Pain at discharge" and "pain at 6 weeks" are different outcomes. Pre-specify which you will pool.

3.2 Risk of bias vs. quality vs. reporting

ConceptQuestion it asks
Risk of biasCould the result be systematically wrong because of how the study was designed or run?
Quality of reportingDid the authors describe what they did? (CONSORT)
PrecisionHow wide is the confidence interval? (a random-error issue, not bias)
High-yield

Bias = systematic error. Imprecision = random error. A small, perfectly run RCT is unbiased but imprecise. A huge, badly run one is precise but biased.

3.3 RoB 2: randomised trials

RoB 2 is the current Cochrane tool. It judges a specific result (one outcome from one trial), not the trial as a whole, and sorts each of its 5 domains into Low risk · Some concerns · High risk through signalling questions.

Reviewer trap
  • Giving each study a single overall score from an old checklist and adding up points. Domain-based judgement is the standard.

Day 4 · Analysis

Pool it.

A meta-analysis is a weighted average of study results, plus an honest account of how much those results disagree.

Effect sizesWeightsForest plotI² · τ² · PI
knowing when NOT to pool is a skill.

4.1 The idea in one line

Definition

Pooled effect = Σ(wᵢ × yᵢ) / Σwᵢ, where yᵢ is each study's effect (log RR, MD, …) and wᵢ is its weight. Inverse-variance weight: wᵢ = 1 / vᵢ, so precise studies count more. Variance vᵢ = SE².

Effect measures at a glance

DataEffectNull valueScale pooled on
BinaryRR / OR1log
BinaryRD0natural
ContinuousMD / SMD0natural
SurvivalHR1log
Clinical pearl

OR ≠ RR when the outcome is common. If baseline risk is high, an OR exaggerates the effect when read as a risk ratio. Prefer RR (or RD) unless the design forces OR. Report absolute effects for the clinician.

4.2 Fixed vs random: it's about what you believe

Common-effect ("fixed")Random-effects
AssumesOne true effect; studies differ only by chanceTrue effects vary across studies (mean μ, spread τ²)
Weights1/vᵢ1/(vᵢ + τ²) — small studies gain weight
CINarrowerWider when heterogeneous
Answers"What is the effect in these studies?""What is the average effect across settings?"
High-yield

Don't choose the model by looking at I². Choose by what the question and the clinical diversity imply. In real life studies differ, so random-effects is the usual default; pre-specify it. Use fixed-effect as a sensitivity check.

How to read it, line by line

  • Direction: pooled RR < 1 → fewer infections with X.
  • Fixed vs random disagree on significance (fixed CI excludes 1; random touches it). That's a flag: heterogeneity is changing the answer. Report it plainly; don't pick the nicer one.
  • Trial C points the other way (RR > 1) and has the biggest fixed-effect weight; random-effects cuts its weight from 36% to 25%.
  • Prediction interval [0.23, 2.00] is wide: in a new setting the true effect might be a big benefit or harm. The CI for the mean alone would hide that.
Mnemonic

Q · τ² · I² · PI

Q tests if there's more spread than chance. τ² = how much true effects vary (in log units). I² = % of the observed spread that's real, not chance. PI = where the next study's true effect likely falls.

Interpreting I² (Cochrane guidance, overlapping bands)

I²Rough reading
0–40%might not be important
30–60%may represent moderate heterogeneity
50–90%may represent substantial heterogeneity
75–100%considerable heterogeneity

Bands deliberately overlap: interpret I² with its CI, the size and direction of effects, and the strength of evidence for heterogeneity (Q p-value). With few studies, Q is underpowered and I² is imprecise.

Reviewer trap
  • "I² = 0 so studies are homogeneous." No: with k = 6 you simply can't tell.
  • Calling a result "significant" because the fixed-effect model says so while random-effects doesn't.
  • Pooling apples and oranges: different populations, doses or outcomes, just because the software allows it.
  • Rare events / zero cells: use Mantel-Haenszel or Peto (careful), and state the continuity-correction rule. Don't silently add 0.5.

4.4 When NOT to pool

  • Clinical diversity you can't defend (different diseases, doses, outcome definitions).
  • Only 1 study per comparison (pooling needs ≥ 2).
  • Heterogeneity so extreme that an average is meaningless → narrative synthesis (SWiM), or explain with subgroups first.

4.5 Doing it in software

# R (free) · package "meta"
library(meta)
m <- metabin(event.e, n.e, event.c, n.c, studlab = study, data = d,

Day 5 · Analysis

Stress-test it.

A pooled number that has never been attacked is a pooled number you don't understand. Today you try to break your own result, then grade how much you trust it.

SubgroupsSensitivityPublication biasGRADE
if it survives your attack, it earns the conclusion.

5.1 Explaining heterogeneity

ToolQuestionRules
Subgroup analysisDoes the effect differ between categories (e.g. adults vs children)?Pre-specify (≤ 3–5), test the difference between subgroups (not "significant here, not there"), few studies per subgroup → low power
Meta-regressionDoes effect change with a continuous covariate (age, dose, year)?Rule of thumb ≥ 10 studies per covariate; observational by nature
Sensitivity analysisDoes the conclusion survive different choices?Pre-specified list; report all results
Reviewer trap
  • "Significant in men, not in women, therefore different." Wrong. Only a formal interaction (subgroup difference) test answers that.
  • Dredging 12 subgroups after seeing the data → false positives. Label post-hoc as exploratory.
  • Between-study comparisons are confounded; subgroup findings do not prove causation.

Standard sensitivity checks

  • Leave-one-out: drop each study in turn; does the estimate or I² collapse? (Trial C in Day 4's example.)
  • Exclude high-RoB studies.
  • Fixed vs random; DL vs REML; with/without HKSJ.
  • Exclude studies with imputed SDs or extreme sizes.
  • Alternative effect measure (RR vs OR vs RD).
High-yield

If the conclusion flips under reasonable alternatives, say so and downgrade certainty (inconsistency / risk of bias). A robust result survives; a fragile one is reported as fragile.

5.2 Publication bias and small-study effects

Symmetric: no obvious asymmetry
Asymmetric: small "negative" studies missing?
  • Funnel plot: effect (x) vs precision/SE (y, inverted). Big precise studies at the top; small noisy ones spread at the bottom.
  • Egger's regression tests asymmetry. Use funnel/Egger/Begg only when there are ≥ 10 studies; fewer gives too little power to tell chance from asymmetry.
  • Trim-and-fill: estimates how many studies are "missing" and adjusts. Use as sensitivity, not truth.
  • Contour-enhanced funnels mark significance zones, helping to separate publication bias from other causes.
Mnemonic

HERO

Asymmetry has causes besides publication bias: Heterogeneity · Effect differs with study size (true) · Reporting bias · Other (chance, artefacts of the effect measure, poor methodological quality in small trials).

Reviewer trap

"Funnel looks symmetric, so no publication bias" with k = 6. You can't conclude that. Say the test is not interpretable; then rely on registry searches (are there registered but unpublished trials?) and grey literature.

5.3 GRADE: how much do you trust the pooled number?

GRADE rates certainty of evidence for each outcome — not each study. Start from the design, then move down for problems and up for strengths.

StartLevel
Randomised trialsHigh ⊕⊕⊕⊕
Observational (non-randomised)Low ⊕⊕○○ (ROBINS-I-based reviews can start high)

Downgrade (5)

  1. Risk of bias
  2. Inconsistency (heterogeneity unexplained)
  3. Indirectness (P/I/C/O differ from your question)
  4. Imprecision (wide CI, small total N / events)
  5. Publication bias

Each: not serious (0), serious (−1), very serious (−2).

Upgrade (3) — mainly observational

  1. Large effect (e.g. RR > 2 or < 0.5)
  2. Dose–response gradient
  3. All plausible confounding would reduce the effect (or suggest a spurious effect when none is seen)

Don't upgrade if you already downgraded for serious bias.

Mnemonic

RIIPP ↓   LDC ↑

Down: Risk of bias · Inconsistency · Indirectness · Imprecision (+) Publication bias. Up: Large effect · Dose-response · Confounding works against effect.

Final certaintyPlain-language meaning
HighWe are very confident the true effect is close to the estimate.
ModerateTrue effect is probably close, but may be substantially different.
LowOur confidence is limited; the true effect may be substantially different.
Very lowWe have very little confidence; the true effect is likely substantially different.

Summary of Findings (SoF) table: the page clinicians read

One row per important outcome: relative effect + CI, absolute effect per 1,000, no. of participants (studies), certainty rating with footnotes explaining each downgrade. Build it free in GRADEpro GDT.

Clinical pearl

Worked thinking, Day 4 example: RCTs start High. Some trials unblinded with subjective outcomes? −1 RoB. I² 45% with trials pointing opposite directions and a wide prediction interval? −1 inconsistency. CI crossing 1 plus possible harm? −1 imprecision. Final: Low, and you must say why.

Reviewer trap
  • Calling a "significant" p-value "high-quality evidence". Different questions.
  • Rating down without writing the reason in a footnote.
  • Applying GRADE to the whole review instead of outcome by outcome.

Day 6 · Analysis

Go network.

Pairwise MA answers "A vs B". Real clinicians face A, B, C, D and a placebo, with trials that never met each other. NMA uses the web of comparisons to rank them, if the web is honest.

GeometryTransitivityConsistencyLeague tableCINeMA
rankings never walk alone.

6.1 The core idea

Definition

Direct evidence = trials that compare A with B head to head. Indirect evidence = A vs B inferred through a common comparator: d(A,B) = d(A,Pl) − d(B,Pl). Mixed (network) estimate = weighted combination of both.

  • Network gains precision and allows comparisons nobody trialled.
  • It adds assumptions. That's where reviewers attack.
High-yield

Pairwise first, network last. If your pairwise MAs are messy, the network will be messier. Build the geometry from the same included studies you've already extracted.

6.2 Geometry and vocabulary

TermMeaning
NodeOne treatment (or defined treatment class)
EdgeDirect comparison made in ≥ 1 trial
Multi-arm trial3+ arms; contributes correlated comparisons: software must handle it
Closed loopBoth direct and indirect evidence for a comparison
Connected networkEvery node is reachable. Disconnected networks cannot be analysed together
ReferenceCommon comparator (placebo / standard care); results often relative to it
Reviewer trap
  • Node definition ("lumping vs splitting"): is "statin" one node or six doses/types? Define in the protocol, before looking at results.
  • Including a treatment that is never used in the clinical decision you care about. Network must be a decision set.
  • Showing the plot with equal-thickness lines. Size nodes by participants, edges by number of trials.

6.3 Assumption 1: Transitivity

Definition

Transitivity: studies comparing A–Pl and B–Pl are similar enough in effect modifiers (age, severity, baseline risk, dose, duration, setting) that an indirect A-vs-B comparison is valid. It's a clinical judgement about study characteristics; you assess it before looking at statistics.

  • Tabulate effect-modifier distributions per comparison (a "transitivity table").
  • If A–Pl trials are in severe disease and B–Pl trials in mild disease, the indirect A vs B is confounded by severity.
  • Nothing substitutes for judgement. Statistical tests can't prove it.

6.4 Assumption 2: Consistency (coherence)

Where direct and indirect evidence both exist, they should agree. Disagreement = inconsistency / incoherence.

LevelMethodQuestion
GlobalDesign-by-treatment interaction testAnywhere in the network, is there inconsistency?
LocalNode-splitting (SIDE) / loop inconsistencyFor this comparison, direct vs indirect: do they differ?
Mnemonic

T before C

Transitivity is the assumption (judge by thinking). Consistency is the testable consequence (check by statistics). If transitivity fails, consistency tests may or may not catch it.

Reviewer trap

"Test for inconsistency not significant → no inconsistency." Same lesson as I²: tests are underpowered. Present node-split estimates, not just p-values, and take clinical context into account.

6.5 Heterogeneity in a network

Most NMA models estimate a common τ² for all comparisons (an assumption). Heterogeneity within each pairwise comparison should still be examined (Day 4 skills). Network meta-regression can adjust for an effect modifier; needs enough trials.

6.6 Frequentist vs Bayesian

FrequentistBayesian
Typical toolsR netmeta, Stata network suiteR gemtc / BUGSnet (JAGS/Stan)

6.7 League tables and rankings

OutputWhat it shows
League tableEvery pairwise effect (with CI/CrI) in one matrix. The primary result.
Forest plot vs referenceAll treatments vs placebo
SUCRA / P-score0–100%: average rank, higher = more likely better. Not an effect size.
RankogramProbability of each treatment being 1st, 2nd, …
High-yield

Rankings must always appear next to the estimates and their certainty. A treatment can rank first on a trivial effect with a CI covering benefit and harm. SUCRA of 90% in a sparse, imprecise network is noise with a nice number on it.

Reviewer trap
  • "Treatment X is the best" written from SUCRA alone.
  • Ranking drugs that have few trials with other drugs that have many.
  • No statement of the clinical importance threshold. "Different" is not "better".

6.8 CINeMA: confidence in network estimates

CINeMA (Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis) is a free web app that rates each network estimate using 6 domains, then gives High / Moderate / Low / Very low.

#DomainIdea

3IndirectnessStudies' P/I/C/O vs your target question

5HeterogeneityPrediction interval vs clinical thresholds
6IncoherenceDirect vs indirect disagreement (node-split), plus overall consistency

Within-study bias · Reporting bias · Indirectness · Imprecision · Heterogeneity · Incoherence. Six boxes: tick each for no concerns / some / major.

Alternative: GRADE extended to NMA (Puhan et al. four-step approach). Cite whichever you use; CINeMA is the most practical for beginners because it is stepwise and software-supported.

7.2 Structure of the final report

SectionMust include
Title"Systematic review and meta-analysis / network meta-analysis" in the title
AbstractStructured per PRISMA 2020 abstract: objectives, eligibility, sources, RoB, synthesis, results, limitations, funding, registration
IntroductionRationale; objectives in PICO form
MethodsRegistration + protocol + deviations; eligibility; sources + dates; full strategy; selection; extraction; RoB; effect measures; synthesis; heterogeneity; sensitivity; reporting bias; certainty
ResultsFlow diagram; characteristics table; RoB; forest plots; heterogeneity; sensitivity; certainty / SoF; for NMA: network plot, league table, ranks with CINeMA
DiscussionSummary; comparison with other evidence; limitations of evidence and review; implications for practice and research
OtherRegistration number, funding, competing interests, data/code availability

Writing rules for the Results and Discussion

  • Results report; the Discussion interprets. Don't mix.
  • Report every pre-specified analysis, including null and unflattering ones.
  • Use certainty language that matches GRADE/CINeMA: "may reduce" for low, "probably reduces" for moderate.
  • Avoid "proves", "no effect" (use "no clear evidence of an effect" when the CI includes meaningful benefit and harm).
Reviewer trap
  • Conclusion stronger than the certainty rating.
  • No "deviations from protocol" statement even though methods changed.
  • Only reporting what's significant. Selective reporting inside a review about selective reporting.
  • Missing limitations of the review itself (language, database coverage, few studies).

7.3 Appraising a published review: AMSTAR 2

AMSTAR 2 (16 items) lets you judge another review. Critical items include: protocol registered before the review; adequate search; justified exclusions; RoB assessed; appropriate pooling; RoB considered when interpreting; publication bias assessed. Overall confidence: High / Moderate / Low / Critically low.

7.4 The capstone: your protocol (template)

PROTOCOL · SR / MA / NMA

  1. Title — "[Intervention] vs [comparator] for [outcome] in [population]: a systematic review and meta-analysis (protocol)".
  2. Registration — PROSPERO ID (or OSF link); authors and roles; amendments log; funding; competing interests.
  3. Background and rationale — what is known, what is missing, why an SR now (≈250 words).
  4. Objectives — PICO sentence.
  5. Eligibility — table: P, I, C, O (primary + secondary + time point), S, language, dates.
  6. Information sources — databases (MEDLINE, Embase, CENTRAL…), registries, grey literature, citation chasing; planned search date; full draft strategy in appendix.
  7. Selection process — dedup tool, 2 independent reviewers, Rayyan, conflict resolution, pilot, flow diagram.
  8. Data extraction — items, form, pilot, 2 reviewers, author contact, handling multi-arm and multiple reports.
  9. Risk of bias — RoB 2 / ROBINS-I; independent; how judgements feed synthesis.
  10. Effect measures — RR/OR/MD/SMD/HR; handling zero events; medians-to-means.
  11. Synthesis — random-effects, τ² estimator, CI method, software; criteria for pooling; narrative if not pooled.
  12. Heterogeneity / subgroups / sensitivity — pre-specified list, with direction and reason.
  13. Reporting bias — funnel/Egger only if ≥ 10 studies; registry comparison.
  14. NMA only — node definition, transitivity assessment, model (freq/Bayes), consistency checks, ranking presentation, CINeMA.
  15. Certainty — GRADE (or CINeMA) per outcome; SoF outcomes listed.
  16. Timeline and dissemination — milestones, planned journal type, data sharing.
Clinical pearl

Run a pre-mortem. Ask a friend to read your protocol and write the reviewer comment they would give. If three comments repeat — fix the protocol, not your defence.

7.5 Authorship, ethics, honesty

  • Authorship follows ICMJE criteria: substantial contribution, drafting/revising, approval, accountability. Writing a paper for someone else's name doesn't qualify them as author.
  • SRs of published data usually don't need ethics committee review, but check your institution's rule and state it.
  • Disclose any AI-assisted tools you used and keep human responsibility for every claim and number. Do not let a tool invent citations.
  • Avoid predatory journals: check indexing (e.g. PubMed/Scopus coverage) and peer-review practice.
