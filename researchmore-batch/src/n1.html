Every review that fails, fails here. A vague question gives a vague search, a vague pool and a vague paper. Spend today making the question sharp enough to hurt.

1.1 What are we actually doing?

DesignWhat it isOutputUse when
Narrative reviewAuthor picks and summarises papers; no fixed methodOpinion with citationsTeaching, background. Not evidence.
Systematic review (SR)Pre-specified question, reproducible search, 2 reviewers, critical appraisalStructured synthesisAlways the foundation
Meta-analysis (MA)Statistical pooling of results inside an SROne pooled estimate + CIStudies are similar enough to combine
Network MA (NMA)MA of ≥3 treatments at once using direct + indirect evidenceEvery pairwise effect + rankingMany options, few head-to-head trials
Scoping reviewMaps what exists; no pooling, no RoB neededEvidence mapBroad, exploratory questions
High-yield

Every MA should sit inside an SR. Not every SR needs an MA. A meta-analysis without a systematic search is just arithmetic on a convenient sample of papers.

Where it sits in the evidence hierarchy

Single case → case series → case-control → cohort → RCT → SR/MA of RCTs (top). Garbage-in applies: a pooled estimate from biased trials is a precise wrong answer. That is why Days 3 and 5 exist.

BadWhy it failsSharpened

Reviewer trap
  • Outcome = "outcomes". Name the primary outcome, the measure and the time point. Switching primary outcome after seeing results is outcome-switching: the cardinal sin.
  • Population too broad ("all patients") → heterogeneity you can never explain. Too narrow → 2 studies.
  • Comparator left vague: "standard care" varies by country and decade. Define it.

1.3 Eligibility criteria

Inclusion and exclusion criteria are the fence round your review. Write them before searching. Each rule needs a reason a reviewer can accept.

Clinical pearl

A language restriction ("English only") is the commonest unforced error. Excluding non-English trials can shift effect estimates and it must be listed as a limitation.

1.5 The protocol and PRISMA-P

Definition

Protocol = the written plan, fixed before you screen, stating question, eligibility, search, methods of appraisal, synthesis and what you will do if the data are awkward. PRISMA-P (2015) is the 17-item checklist for what a protocol must contain.

Why bother? It stops you choosing methods after peeking at results, it exposes duplicate work, and journals and reviewers increasingly ask for it.

Protocol sectionWhat goes in it
AdministrativeTitle ("systematic review"/"meta-analysis"), registration, authors and roles, amendments, funding
IntroductionRationale, objectives in PICO form
Methods: eligibilityPICOS, years, languages, report status

Methods: appraisalRoB tool, how judgements are used
Methods: synthesisEffect measure, model, heterogeneity, subgroups, sensitivity, publication bias, certainty (GRADE)

Registering on PROSPERO

  • PROSPERO is a free international registry of systematic reviews with a health-related outcome, maintained by the Centre for Reviews and Dissemination, University of York.
  • Register before you start screening; it is meant to record plans, not completed work. Registration is a record, not peer review or an endorsement.
  • Needs: a title, review question, search plan, eligibility, primary outcome, RoB method, synthesis plan. Keep these identical to your protocol.
  • Not eligible (typically scoping reviews, narrative reviews)? A public time-stamped protocol on OSF Registries is the fallback.
Reviewer trap

Registration after results is theatre. If methods changed, say so in a "deviations from protocol" paragraph and update the registration. Honest change is fine. Hidden change is misconduct.

Hands-on · Day 1 deliverable
  1. Write your topic as a single PICO sentence + one primary outcome with time point.
  2. Search PROSPERO + PubMed for existing reviews; paste the 3 closest and write one line each on how yours differs.
  3. Fill the eligibility table (above) with a reason in every row.
  4. Draft the protocol headings from PRISMA-P and write Introduction + Objectives (150–250 words).

Practice MCQs

1. Which element of PICO is most often left vague, leading to outcome switching?
A. Population B. Intervention C. Comparator D. Outcome
Answer D. Undefined outcomes (what, how measured, at what time) let authors choose the "best-looking" one later.
2. The best time to register a systematic review is:
A. After data extraction B. After analysis C. Before screening begins D. At manuscript submission
Answer C. Registration records the plan; doing it after results defeats the purpose.
3. PRISMA-P is a reporting guideline for:
A. The completed review B. The review protocol C. Search strategies D. Network meta-analyses
Answer B. PRISMA 2020 = completed review; PRISMA-S = searches; PRISMA-NMA = network reviews.
4. A question about harms of an environmental exposure in cohort studies is best framed as:
A. PICO B. PECO C. SPIDER D. PCC
Answer B. Exposure replaces Intervention when you do not assign it.
5. A meta-analysis without a systematic search is best described as:
A. Acceptable if studies are RCTs B. A narrative review with maths C. A scoping review D. An NMA
Answer B. Selective inclusion biases the pooled estimate however good the statistics.
6. Which restriction needs the strongest justification?
A. Parallel-group RCTs only B. Adults only C. English-language only D. Confirmed diagnosis
Answer C. Language limits can bias estimates and are rarely driven by the question.
7. PROSPERO accepts registration of:
A. Any literature review B. Systematic reviews with a health-related outcome C. Completed papers only D. Primary RCTs
Answer B. It is a registry of SRs, not trials (trials go to CTRI/ClinicalTrials.gov).
8. Pooled log-RR is back-transformed because RR is:
A. Skewed multiplicative B. Always normal C. Unit-dependent D. Absolute
Answer A. Ratios are symmetric on the log scale; pool there, report on the natural scale.
